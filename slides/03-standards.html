STANDARDS AND COORDINATION

How the industry scores an agentic flaw, how it decides what to do about it, and where a reporter is supposed to send it.

AIVSS v0.8 · the SSVC track · AI CVE and CWE · Akrites · FLARE-AI · the routing tree

Frame the module as three questions in order: how bad is it, how urgently do I act, and where do I send it. Different models answer each one. Conflating them is the most common mistake in this space, and it is the mistake this module is built to prevent.

Question one

AIVSS scores a vulnerability in a deployment context

What CVSS answers

  • How severe is this defect, considered as a defect
  • Stable across everywhere the software is installed

What it cannot answer

  • The same defect reached by an agent with shell, egress and cloud credentials
  • Autonomy, tool reach and persistence are properties of the deployment, not the defect

AIVSS exists to express that amplification as a number. Whether a number is the right instrument is a fair question, and we will come back to it.

AIVSS Scoring System for OWASP Agentic AI Core Security Risks v0.8 [P].

Ken is AIVSS project lead. Say so, and then be visibly harder on AIVSS than an outsider would be. The room's trust depends on hearing the caveats from the person with the most incentive to omit them. The caveats are on slides 12 to 15 and they are all drawn from the framework's own text.

A scoring-design defect

Version 0.5 could make a severe flaw score lower

⚠v0.5 averaged the two scores: AIVSS = ((CVSS_Base + AARS) / 2) × ThM. Averaging a 9.4 CVE with an 8.5 agentic score gives you less than 9.4.
Agentic AI Tool MisuseCVSSAIVSSDirection
Under v0.59.48.7Amplification made it smaller
Under v0.89.49.9Amplification can only add

Teach this as a lesson about building severity models. A model whose purpose is to express amplification must not be able to subtract.

AIVSS v0.5 and v0.8 worked examples, both [P]. Arithmetic in research/w1-aivss.md.

This is the most transferable slide in the module and it is a criticism of our own framework's earlier version. Anyone in the room building an internal risk score has probably made this exact mistake. Ask: who here has a scoring formula with an average in it? Hands go up.

The current model

v0.8 closes a gap rather than averaging

AIVSS v0.8, Sections 3.3, 3.4 and 3.6 [P].

Walk the bar left to right. Blue is what the CVE is worth on its own. Everything to its right is headroom. Factor_Sum/10 is the proportion of that headroom the deployment context closes. Say the key consequence out loud: the model can never reduce a score, and it can never exceed 10.

Factor_Sum

Ten Risk Amplification Factors, scored 0, 0.5 or 1

What the agent can do

  • Autonomy — acts without a human in the loop
  • Tools — breadth of what it can invoke
  • Language — natural-language driven
  • Context — adapts across environments
  • Multi-Agent — collaborates with other agents

How hard it is to see or stop

  • Non-Determinism — behaviour is emergent
  • Opacity — reasoning is not inspectable
  • Persistence — survives across sessions
  • Identity — holds or mints credentials
  • Self-Mod — changes its own behaviour

These describe how the system is deployed. Two organizations running the identical CVE will score it differently, which is the point.

AIVSS v0.8, Section 2.3 [P].

The grouping into capability and observability is ours, not the document's. Say so. It helps people score faster because the second column is usually where they have been optimistic.

ThM

The default sits close to "actively attacked", by design

Exploit maturityThM
Attacked1.00
Proof-of-Concept0.97 (default)
Unreported0.50

The stated reasoning

  • Agentic exploits are often natural language, needing no compilation
  • An offensive agent can ingest a proof of concept and weaponize it autonomously
  • So the disclosure-to-attack interval collapses
⚠This is a design judgment declared by the framework, not a measurement. No published dataset establishes the interval.

Teach it as such

  • The reasoning is plausible and Module 1's calibration data does not contradict it
  • It is also unfalsified rather than verified
  • Anyone adopting AIVSS inherits this assumption whether or not they notice it

AIVSS v0.8, Table 4a and surrounding text [P]. Characterization as a design judgment is ours.

Defaults are policy. A 0.97 default means almost every score is computed as though a working exploit exists. That may well be right for agentic systems, and it is still a choice somebody made that most adopters will never see.

Mitigation_Factor

The best mitigation you can claim is 0.67

StrengthDescriptionValue
No / WeakAbsent or ineffective1.00
PartialIncomplete, not reliably enforceable, or not validated against realistic adversarial conditions0.83
StrongValidated, consistently enforceable, fail-closed where applicable0.67
⚠The 0.67 floor is described in the document as a provisional anchor set by framework design consensus, open to community review and awaiting empirical calibration.

The stated rationale is that no mitigation fully eliminates residual risk from agentic amplification. That is a declared assumption, not a finding.

AIVSS v0.8, Table 4b and surrounding text [P].

Note what this does in practice: perfect controls cannot pull a Critical into High. Some rooms will find that motivating and some will find it demoralizing. Both reactions are reasonable. The honest line is that the number is awaiting calibration and should not be treated as settled.

Reading the table properly

The weaker the CVE, the larger the uplift

AIVSS v0.8, Section 3.6 [P].

The framework says this directly: when AARS uplift substantially exceeds CVSS_Base, the deployment context rather than the technical defect is the primary risk driver. That is the single most useful sentence in the document for an engineering audience, because it tells you what the number is claiming. Goal and Instruction Manipulation at CVSS 2.1 is barely a defect. At AIVSS 7.1 it is a deployment problem.

The honest caution

Seven of ten are Critical. What is that prioritizing?

The problem

  • Seven of the ten core risks land in the Critical band
  • The remaining three are High
  • None is Medium or Low
  • A model that rates almost everything Critical has limited power to rank
⚠Severity is not urgency. That is exactly why the SSVC track exists alongside the score.

What the framework itself flags

  • Section 3.2 is titled "Important Statistical Caveat: Ordinal vs. Interval Scales"
  • Arithmetic on ordinal severity inputs is not fully sound, and the document says so
  • AIVSS scores are not directly comparable to CVSS base scores

Present the caveat in the same breath as the formula. A framework that flags its own limits deserves to have them repeated, not quietly dropped.

AIVSS v0.8, Sections 3.2 and 3.6 [P]. Band distribution counted from the Section 3.6 table.

This is the slide that earns the room's trust. Ken leads this project and is saying the number clusters at the top. If anyone is going to push back on AIVSS, invite it here rather than in the corridor afterwards.

A real friction point

AIVSS wants CVSS v4.0. The corpus is v3.1.

⚠v0.8 specifies CVSS v4.0 as the base input. NVD publishes only a v3.1 score for CVE-2026-65617, the anchor for our capstone: 8.8 HIGH. No v4.0 vector exists for it.

What an assessor actually does

  • Compute a v4.0 vector themselves, or
  • Substitute v3.1 quietly and never mention it

What we do in the capstone

  • Substitute v3.1 and say so on the slide
  • Carry a sensitivity range instead of a single number

A framework that requires inputs most of the corpus does not have is a framework with an adoption problem, not a footnote.

AIVSS v0.8, Section 3.3 [P]; NVD record for CVE-2026-65617 [P]. Full working in research/synthesis-capstone.md.

This one came out of actually trying to score a real incident rather than reading the document. That is worth saying: the friction is invisible until you use the framework in anger. Flag that the capstone shows the sensitivity table, and that a 3.3-point swing in the input moves the output by only 0.5, which is a genuine strength of the gap-closing design.

Question two

The SSVC track answers urgency, not severity

Inputs

  • P(Threat) — exploitation state
  • P(Vulnerability) — exploit success probability
  • Impact — systemic consequence
  • Agent Level — exposure multiplier: Copilot 2×, Specialist 4×, Prime Mover 8×

Outputs

  • Defer
  • Scheduled
  • Out-of-Cycle
  • Immediate
⚠The AIVSS track scores its ten factors 0 / 0.5 / 1. The SSVC track scores its ten factors 1 to 5. They are not the same ten factors and the numbers are not interchangeable.

AIVSS-SSVC calculator, OWASP AIVSS Project [P]. Scale mismatch observed during the capstone working.

Say the scale mismatch out loud and slowly. It is exactly the kind of detail that produces confidently wrong answers in practice, because both are "ten factors" and both come from the same project.

New institution, June 2026

Akrites: a shared SIRT for critical open source

What it is

  • Co-founded by the Linux Foundation and OpenSSF, announced 25 June 2026
  • 19 named founding organizations
  • Seed funding from Alpha-Omega
  • One standardized CVD process, confidentiality first
  • Built on CVE, TLP, CWE, CVSS, EPSS, SSVC and VEX

Three commitments that define it

  • Success is measured in patch deployment, not patch publication
  • A predictable partner for maintainers rather than a flood of uncoordinated reports
  • Maintainer of last resort for packages with no active maintainer

Linux Foundation press release, 2026-06-25 [P]. Secondary coverage saying "over 20 founding members" does not match the 19 named in the release.

The first commitment is the interesting one and it is a rebuke to how the industry currently measures itself. Publishing a patch is not the outcome; a deployed patch is. If the room takes one institutional idea away from this module, make it that one.

A question worth raising, not smoothing over

Maintainer of last resort, without fragmenting the ecosystem

The release commits that fixes flow back to each project's original home, on maintainers' terms, and that the initiative will not bypass maintainers or fragment the ecosystem with proprietary solutions. It also commits to acting as maintainer of last resort where no maintainer is active.

⚠Those two commitments sit in tension. Who decides that a maintainer is inactive, and what happens when that maintainer disagrees?

Linux Foundation press release, 2026-06-25 [P]. The tension is our reading, not a stated position.

Mark this clearly as our reading. It is not a criticism, and Akrites may well have an answer that simply is not in the press release. Good open-floor question if the room includes maintainers, because they will have strong views.

New mechanism, June 2026

FLARE-AI: flaw reporting built for model behaviour

What it is

  • arXiv 2606.31567, submitted 30 June 2026
  • Longpre and 17 co-authors, 18 total
  • Audited 12 existing flaw reporting systems
  • Consulted 49 experts across 32 organizations

Mechanism

  • Collects triage information through conditional logic with early classification
  • Optionally disseminates a standardized machine-readable report to multiple developers

Five design challenges it names

  • Discoverability
  • Scope
  • Information collection
  • Coordination
  • Guidance for strict-liability cases
⚠Adoption and volume numbers are unpublished. A reporter choosing FLARE-AI in 2026 is choosing on stated intent.

FLARE-AI, Longpre et al., arXiv 2606.31567, 2026-06-30 [P]; MIT AI Risk Initiative announcement [P].

The multi-developer dissemination is the genuinely new capability. Every other destination is one-to-one. A behavioural flaw that affects six providers currently means six separate reports, or five that never get filed.

The corpus

The AI weakness gap is acknowledged and still open

What exists

  • A CWE Artificial Intelligence Working Group, established by CWE and CVE community stakeholders
  • Scope: identify and address gaps where AI-related weaknesses are not adequately covered
  • A CVE-AI-Discussion subgroup that resolves

What does not

  • No AI-specific CWE entries are listed. No published or proposed AI-related CWE IDs on the page
  • The gap is acknowledged institutionally and not yet closed in the corpus

This matters more than it sounds. Without weakness classes that describe agent failures, a vulnerability record has nothing to point at.

CWE community working groups page, MITRE [P], read 2026-08-27.

Question three

Nine destinations. No decision procedure.

⚠So we built one. What follows is an original proposal assembled from each destination's own stated scope. It is a starting position for discussion, not established practice.

Our proposal

Four branches, and one that nothing serves

research/synthesis-routing-tree.md. Built from each destination's stated scope [P]; the routing logic is ours.

The most common routing error is treating every AI-related finding as an AI flaw. A memory-safety bug in an inference server is an ordinary vulnerability that happens to live in AI infrastructure, and it routes like any other CVE. CVE-2026-4747 is the proof: found by an AI system, routed through FreeBSD's own security team, produced an ordinary advisory. The discovery method changed nothing.

A structural problem, not a villain

Who explains, and who waits

Hugging Face

  • A short update and a promise of more

The organization that was intruded upon had every incentive to explain. The organization whose agent did the intruding had every incentive to wait. Any coordination body inherits that asymmetry, and neither Akrites nor FLARE-AI has published a mechanism that changes the incentive.

Resist making this a morality slide. It is an incentive-design observation and it generalizes: whoever is harmed publishes, whoever caused it waits for counsel. If the room wants to argue about it, that is a good use of five minutes.

Module 3 in five lines

What to take back to work

  • Severity, urgency and routing are three different questions. Do not let one number answer all three.
  • If your internal risk model contains an average, check that amplification cannot make a score go down.
  • Read the defaults. A 0.97 threat multiplier and a 0.67 mitigation floor are policy decisions you inherit silently.
  • When a framework wants inputs your corpus does not have, say which substitution you made and show a sensitivity range.
  • Decide your routing before you find something. The worst time to design a disclosure process is during a disclosure.

Drawn from AIVSS v0.8 [P], research/w2-ecosystem.md, and synthesis-routing-tree.md.

Five lines, no new material. If you are running short, this is the slide to land on, and the capstone can be shortened instead.
